# The Hundred Dollar Truth

"Honestly? It tastes like a wet sock."

The whole table freezes. 14 relatives. One fork stops halfway to a mouth. Grandma Rosa's casserole sits in the middle, steaming, proud.

Something flutters down from the ceiling fan. A $100 bill. It lands on the casserole.

Your cousin Kofi whispers, "Did you just get paid for that?" Your mom puts her face in her hands. Grandma Rosa blinks twice, picks up the bill, and holds it to the light.

"It's real," she says. "I hate that it's real."

Since this morning, every time you tell a truth that scares you, a hundred dollar bill drops into your hand. Fresh. Crisp. Smells like ink. Tell the right truths and you pay for lunch this week. Keep going and you pay for college by spring. Keep going and you buy your mom a house. And one afternoon, the truth you owe someone will cost more than any bill can pay.

For now, you're up $100 and down one grandmother.

Most people lie once or twice a day. In a famous diary study, that's what people wrote down, about one or two lies every single day. Scientists even have a name for why it works. It's called the truth-default. We assume people are honest, unless something looks off.

You're about to look very off.

There are rules, and you learn them fast. It only pays for truths that scare you. Say "it's Tuesday" and you get nothing. Tell a lie, nothing. Tell a half-truth, nothing. The money knows. And the price never changes. Always $100. Never a cent more.

Monday, history class. The teacher asks who did the reading. Twenty-eight hands go up. You keep yours down. "I didn't." Silence. A bill lands on your desk and slides onto the floor. She pretends not to see it. She gives you a detention. It's the best $100 you ever made.

Your friend Dana walks up after class, touching her new bangs. "Do I look good?" Your stomach drops. You say it anyway. "They make you look like a startled lamp." She stares. Then she bursts out laughing. "I HATE them. I thought it was just me." $100 on the sidewalk. She helps you pick it up.

Researchers have actually tested this. People told to have honest, deeper conversations expected them to be awkward. They were far less awkward than expected. People enjoyed them more than they predicted. Honesty is scarier in your head than it is in real life.

At practice, Coach Brandt makes the team run in the sun. Everybody calls him Coach No-Excuses. His favorite rule is on a banner above the gym door. Water is a reward. He asks you how you like practice. "Honestly, I hate it." A bill falls out of your sleeve. He makes you run extra laps. You earn $100 and a blister.

The linebacker next to you, Colm, never complains. He's six foot three and cracked a finger last month. Coach asked if he was hurt. Colm said, "I'm good!" He says it for everything. A sprained ankle. A cut lip. A fever. Everyone knows it's his catchphrase. Nobody thinks about what it means.

Sunday, you apologize to Grandma Rosa. "The casserole is terrible. But your lasagna is the best thing I've ever eaten." Another bill. She laughs so hard she needs to sit down. She's a retired nurse. She spent 30 years in the desert and she still tells those stories at every dinner. "In heat, the only thing that matters is cold," she says. "Cool them first. Ambulance second. Ice in the tub." You nod. You don't know why you remember it.

By day five, you have $2,400. Your mom finds it in your sock drawer. "Where did this come from?" You tell her the truth. "I tell the truth." She laughs. Then she realizes you're serious. Then you tell her you stole a cookie in third grade, and another bill drops. She leaves the room.

You buy pizza for the whole class. You pay Dana back for bangs-related emotional damage. You buy your mom a new coat. She cries. You've told 11 truths this week, and you've never felt this light.

It stops being a game at the school assembly. 900 students. The principal asks if anyone has questions. Everyone stares at their shoes. You stand up. Your knees are shaking. "The fish sticks in the cafeteria. They expired on Monday. I read the box." Gasps. Principal Ibrahim goes pale. By lunch, the fish sticks are gone. By Friday, the cafeteria has a new supplier. $100 in your hand. Applause behind you.

A local reporter finds you outside school. "Are you scared right now?" "Yes." $100. The segment airs that night. They call you the Truth Kid. You get 14,000 followers before breakfast.

Then the city council. A bridge on the east side was signed off by an inspector who never actually visited it. You know because your uncle works there, and he said it once at dinner with a laugh. You stand up in a room of 60 adults and say it out loud. Your voice cracks. The bridge is closed that week. Inspectors find cracks under the deck. It's repaired by spring. A man in the back stands up and shakes your hand without saying anything.

You make a hundred bucks. The truth might have saved someone's life. Same price.

Now strangers line up outside your house. "What do you honestly think of me?" They hold out their hands and wait. You tell a woman her business plan won't work. You tell a man his poem is boring. Some cry. Some thank you. Some pay you to keep going. A famous YouTuber flies in to ask if his new video is any good. You say no. You get $100. He says it's the nicest thing anyone has done for him all year.

A lawyer friend of your dad tells you something that stops you cold. In the US, people who report huge corporate fraud to the SEC can collect between 10% and 30% of the fines. When the fines top a million dollars, that's a lot of money. You've told the truth that closed a bridge. You were paid like you told someone their tie was ugly.

The price is flat. Courage and cruelty cost exactly the same.

So you start doing the math. If every scary truth is $100, why bother with the hard ones? There's a girl at school who sings off-key. A teacher who wears a toupee. A neighbor whose lawn looks like a crime scene. Telling people these things is scary enough. Day twenty, you're making $3,000 a day. You've said 30 truths before lunch. You've also made a seventh grader cry. You gave her $100 for the trouble.

Psychologists call this moral licensing. After you've done something good, your brain feels like you've earned a little cruelty. You told the truth about the bridge. So you can tell a stranger his tie is hideous. You've paid in advance.

The bill shows up quietly.

First, people stop asking your opinion. Not because they're angry. Because they can't tell what you're doing. Dana catches you on the walk home. "You said my new jacket looked like a garbage bag. Was that real, or were you farming?" You open your mouth. You can't answer. You don't know.

Second, you start hunting. Every conversation becomes a search for the ugliest true thing. You miss the good ones. Your friend finishes a marathon and you notice his shoes are untied. His mom hugs you at the door and you notice her hair is thinning. Kind truths pay nothing, so you stop saying them.

Third, you can't enjoy it. A truth you're paid for is a job. You sit in your room with a stack of cash and nobody to spend it with. Grandma Rosa calls on Sunday to ask if you're coming to dinner. You say you'll try. That's a lie. No bill. You don't mind. At least a lie is free.

You've become a person who says what he thinks and means nothing by it.

August. Two-a-day football practice. You joined as the kicker, because the team needed one and you needed something that wasn't about words. Coach Brandt has the whole squad on the field at 3 p.m. It's 98 degrees. The sky is white. The grass is crunchy.

Colm is on the far end of the sideline. His helmet is off. He's walking in a slow circle. Coach blows the whistle for another round of sprints. Colm lines up anyway.

You watch him run twice and stumble. His face is red. Then gray. He stops sweating. You can see the line of dry skin on his neck. Somebody asks, "Colm, you okay?"

"I'm good."

His words are slurred. He's looking at the wrong teammate. He asks what day it is. Then he asks why you're at his house.

You know what this is. Grandma Rosa made sure of it.

Heat stroke. Once a body hits about 40 degrees Celsius, 104 Fahrenheit, with confusion and collapse, it's a life-threatening emergency. It's one of the leading causes of death in high school athletes. But there's good news. If you cool someone fast, within about 30 minutes, nearly everyone survives. The rule is simple. Cool first. Transport second.

The ambulance is 15 minutes away. The trainer left at noon. Coach Brandt is yelling about effort. Every teammate says the same thing. "He's good. He always says that."

You have two choices. You can say nothing. Say "he's fine, Coach." Keep your spot on the team. Keep your scholarship chance. Keep the peace. Nobody would ever blame you.

Or you can say the one sentence Coach Brandt has promised to punish. He told the team on day one: "Anyone who stops my practice is off my team. Permanent."

You take one step. Then another.

"Coach. Colm is not okay."

"Back in line."

"He's confused. He stopped sweating. This is heat stroke."

"He said he's good."

"He says that when his finger's broken."

The whole field is watching. Coach points at the sideline. "One more word and you're done." You look at him. And you realize you aren't thinking about money at all.

"He needs the ice tub. Now."

Grandma Rosa's voice plays in your head. Cool them first. Ambulance second.

A hundred dollar bill flutters down onto the grass. Nobody sees it. You don't look.

You run. The athletic trainer's room is unlocked. There's a big blue tub of ice by the door. Two linemen help you drag it across the field. You pull Colm's pads off. His jersey. His cleats. His skin is burning. Three teammates lower him in. Ice, water, shoulders. Somebody finally dials 911. Somebody else starts timing it.

Colm gasps. His eyes snap open.

"Stay with me. Say your name."

"Colm."

"Say it again."

"Colm Reyes."

Four minutes. Coach Brandt takes one step toward you, then stops. Seven minutes. A freshman holds the stopwatch with both hands and calls out every 30 seconds. Ten. The ambulance pulls onto the field. Paramedics check a thermometer and look at each other. One of them says quietly, "Ten more minutes and we'd be having a different conversation."

Colm turns his head. His lips are blue. His voice is barely a whisper. "I'm not good."

You laugh. You start to cry. You hold his hand.

Coach Brandt stands by the fence with his whistle hanging off his neck. He hasn't said anything. Finally, he walks over. He takes the banner down himself that night. Water is not a reward.

He does bench you. The school board meets. They add a heat policy to every sport. A water break every 15 minutes. A trainer on the field. A tub on the sideline. Colm's mom hugs you in the parking lot and doesn't let go.

That Sunday, Dana waits on your porch. She doesn't smile. "Real, or farming?" You think about the field. "I didn't even look for the bill." She studies your face for a long time. Then she nods. "Okay."

Colm spends three days in the hospital. On the second one, you visit. He's holding a cup of ice chips like a trophy.

"Ask me how I am."

"How are you?"

"Scared. A little sad. Starving." He grins. "Never said that out loud before."

No bill falls. Maybe the power only pays you. Maybe some truths are free.

The $100 is still on the grass on Monday. Nobody took it. You leave it there.

Grandma Rosa calls. "Are you coming to dinner?" "Yes." No bill. It's the best nothing you've ever earned.

That afternoon, a woman in a rain-stained coat is waiting outside your school. She's the hospital administrator. She slides a folder across the bench. Inside, 23 names. Next to each one, a school, a date, and a number from a thermometer. The highest is 41.7. "Twenty-three coaches in this county. Twenty-three summers. Every one of them says they follow the rules." She taps the folder. "Tell them the truth. Out loud. In person. Every one of them. A hundred dollars a truth."

You laugh. She doesn't.

The scary part isn't the coaches. It's that your first thought was the money. You count it in your head. 2,300 dollars. Before you even said yes.

"Want the job?"

Would you take money for every truth you told, knowing it might turn honesty into work? Tell me in the comments. And would you have stopped that practice, if it cost you the team?
