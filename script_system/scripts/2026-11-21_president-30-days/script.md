# Mr. President

At 6:58 a.m., a four-star general salutes your pajama pants.

Thirty people in suits stand when you walk into the Oval Office. Nobody sits until you do. A woman hands you a folder. Inside, one sheet of paper. Decide before 9:00 a.m.

Your phone buzzes. Sunny, from home. "Bet you last a week."

You're 17. You're holding a bowl of cereal. And you are the president of the United States.

Last night, your name came out of a drum. It's called the Citizen Term. Congress passed it years ago as a dare. One random citizen runs the country for 30 days, and every politician has to watch.

Week one, you sign your first law. Week two, a foreign leader calls you at 3 a.m. By week four, a storm is heading for the coast. And one night, you make a decision that half the room swears is wrong. Nobody holds your hand for that one.

For today, the biggest decision on your desk is dinner. Eight world leaders. Chicken or fish.

A regular person makes choices that change a handful of lives. Their own, their family's. You decide for 340 million. About 3 million civilians work for the government. The White House has 132 rooms and 35 bathrooms, and you can't find any of them.

There are rules. You can't write laws. Congress does that. When a bill lands on your desk, you get 10 days to sign it or veto it, not counting Sundays. Every morning, a folder called the daily brief lists everything that went wrong overnight. Breakfast is optional.

Day one. You pick pizza for the state dinner. Eight leaders, one prime minister asking for pineapple. You say yes. Two of them walk out. A newspaper calls it pizza diplomacy. Your approval rating is 91%.

Day three. You try to sign an order canceling Mondays. A lawyer coughs. "Technically, sir, no." "What if we say it nicely?" "Still no." He writes it down anyway. For the museum.

Day five. You ask the head of the road department about the pothole outside your school. By Friday, 12,000 potholes are fixed across the country. It's the most loved thing you've ever done. Approval: 84%.

Day six. Congress sends a bill. 1,100 pages. The staff says nobody reads those. You read it all night with a flashlight and a highlighter. On page 902, there's a tax break written for exactly one company's parking lot. You veto it. Silence. Then someone laughs. Then everybody does.

Day nine. A small town begs you to visit. You land by helicopter on a football field with 4,000 people in the bleachers. Their only bridge has been closed for two years. A kid asks if you can fix it. You say yes into a live microphone. The crowd chants your name. Ms. Okafor leans over in the helicopter. "You just promised money that doesn't exist." "I'll find it." "You promised before you checked. That's a habit." You wave it away. Approval: 88%.

The chief of staff is Ms. Okafor. She's 71 and she says no to everything. "No" is how she says good morning. She slides an index card across the desk. Before every decision, ask: who pays if I'm wrong? Then call them. You stick it on the fridge in the residence and forget about it.

At the morning brief, a weather expert named Mateus points at a swirl off the coast of Africa. "Hurricane season peaks in September. We watch it every day." "Is that one dangerous?" "It's nothing." He flips the page.

Only one person tells you the truth. Sunny. She texts after the pizza. "That's a dumb idea." After the Monday order. "That's a very dumb idea." You smile every time.

Day twelve. 3:07 a.m. The phone rings. A foreign leader, out of breath. Two fishing fleets, hers and her neighbor's, are five miles apart in the same patch of ocean. If the coast guards touch, it's over. You rub your eyes. You ask a question nobody else in the room has asked. "Why is everyone fishing at the same time?" "Because the fish are there." "So split the days. You get odd days. They get even days." Silence on the line. "That's it?" "That's how my sister and I share the bathroom."

Three days later, both fleets sign it. The newspapers call it the Bathroom Agreement.

Day eighteen. The United Nations. 193 member countries, and every one has an ambassador in the hall. Interpreters in six languages. You walk to the podium with no script. Your hands shake. You talk for four minutes about sharing a bathroom. The hall laughs. Then it stands. Your face is on a stamp in two countries. Approval: 91% again.

Day nineteen. A late-night host asks what your biggest mistake has been so far. "Pineapple." The audience roars. He leans in. "Seriously. What would you do if you were wrong about something big?" You open your mouth. Nothing comes out. The host moves on. You don't.

It stops being fun on day twenty-one. Nobody says no.

It starts small. You mention the room smells like soup, and six aides open windows. You say your tie is ugly. Four of them throw theirs away. So you test it. "Let's paint the White House green." "Brilliant, sir." A man writes it down. He isn't joking.

You fire the economist who told you the pothole plan was too expensive. The next economist agrees with everything. The room gets very polite.

Researchers have a name for this. In a well-known experiment, people who felt powerful were much more likely to draw the letter E on their own forehead so that it faced themselves. Everyone else saw it backwards. Power makes you worse at seeing the world through another person's eyes.

The bill arrives quietly.

First, the people. Sunny stops texting dumb idea. Now she texts "Yes, Mr. President." As a joke. Then not as a joke. Your mom asks whether you've eaten. You realize you've ordered three dinners. Everybody laughs at your jokes. You miss the sound of somebody being unimpressed.

At midnight, you call Sunny. "Tell me something dumb." "Everything you say is very wise, sir." "Stop." "Sorry, sir." You hang up. You hold the phone for a long time.

Second, you. You check your approval rating every hour. 91. 78. 64. 48. You stop asking what's right. You start asking what looks good.

Third, the meaning. You wanted someone to finally listen. Now everyone listens, and nobody tells you anything true. You can't even tell whether you're right.

On day twenty-seven, you ask Ms. Okafor for fewer objections. "As you wish," she says. It sounds like a door closing.

That morning, Mateus's swirl is no longer nothing. It has a name. Hurricane Odalys. It has an eye.

Day twenty-nine, 2:10 a.m. The Situation Room. A map covers one wall. A red cone sits over the coast. Under it, a city of 2 million people. A port. A bridge. A hospital with 300 patients in intensive care.

Mateus is gray in the face. "55% chance of a direct hit. 40 hours out." Three days from landfall, the center of a storm can still be off by about 100 miles. The deadliest part of a hurricane is the water it pushes ashore. Historically, that surge has killed about half of the people who die in these storms. In 2005, Hurricane Katrina killed more than 1,800 people.

But the evacuation for Hurricane Rita, that same year, killed more than 100 people on the road. Heat. Crashes. A bus fire. More than the storm did.

Evacuate, and you might get people killed. Don't, and you might get them killed. Either way, you'll never know what would have happened.

"It's your call, Mr. President," says a general. "Whatever you decide is right." Eleven people at the table nod. Not one says no.

You think of the index card on the fridge. Who pays if I'm wrong?

"The hospital," you say out loud. "Get me whoever runs it."

Dr. Leila Haddad comes on the screen in scrubs. She's been awake for 20 hours. "Mr. President, moving 300 ICU patients takes 30 hours. If you wait, we can't do it."

The advisers murmur. Wait six hours for a better forecast. It's the careful choice. If the storm hits, you tried to be careful. If it turns, you looked wise.

Go now, and if the storm turns, you emptied a city for nothing. You'll be the kid who panicked. Your 48% will become zero. Nobody would ever blame you for waiting.

Ms. Okafor is in the corner. You told her to stop talking. She doesn't say a word. She just looks at you.

"Evacuate," you say. "Now."

You don't order everyone out at once. That's how highways turn into parking lots. You send low-lying zones first, in waves. You reverse the inbound lanes, so every road leads out. It's called contraflow. Helicopters take the sickest patients. Buses roll through the neighborhoods where people don't own cars. Fuel trucks wait at rest stops along the way.

Hour nine. A traffic map shows 90 miles of red. Somebody on the phone says it's a disaster. "It's slow," Dr. Haddad says, "but it's moving." That's the difference between a traffic jam and a trap.

Hour 22. Mateus walks in with his hands shaking. "The storm just shifted. 40 miles east." The room goes quiet. "We might be wrong about everything." You look at the map. At the red cone. At the 20 hours left. "Keep going." You don't say it because you're sure. You say it because stopping now is the only choice you can't undo.

Hour 31. A bus carrying 40 people from a nursing home breaks down on a bridge. A voice on the radio is calm. "We have a flat tire and 40 grandmothers." You send a second bus, then a tow truck, then a police escort. Nobody dies. That's all you ask for, over and over.

Dr. Haddad nods. "Helicopters in 20 minutes."

For 38 hours, you don't sleep. Roads fill. Phones ring. Someone shouts at you on live television. A man in a pickup calls you a coward. Another one says you ruined his weekend.

Hurricane Odalys makes landfall in the night. 40 miles east of the forecast. The surge climbs the harbor wall and spills into the streets. Six feet of water fill the ground floor of Coastal General. The power fails. The last helicopter left 40 minutes before.

Dr. Haddad's voice is hoarse on the speaker. "We moved 312 patients."

Nobody speaks. Then somebody starts crying. Then someone else.

The headlines come in by morning. The kid who emptied a city. The evacuation cost two billion dollars. Three people died in a crash on the highway. You'll remember their names for the rest of your life. Approval: 31%.

At the press room, you stand at the podium. You don't read the approval rating. You read three names. A man, a woman, and a teenager who was driving his mom to a shelter. When you're done, nobody asks a question for a very long time.

That evening, eleven aides knock on your door together. The general goes first. He takes off his cap. "Sir, in the Situation Room I said whatever you decide is right. I said it because I was afraid. I thought the same thing as you, and I let you carry it alone." One by one, the others say the same thing. Mateus apologizes for the cone. The economist you fired has flown back on his own. He just stands there, holding his hat.

You don't make a speech. You pull out eleven chairs.

Two days later, Dr. Haddad sends a photo. Bus 14, parked outside a shelter. A tired couple in the doorway. In the mother's arms is a baby, born on that bus in the second hour of the evacuation. Her name is Odalys. "The only good thing that storm made," Dr. Haddad writes. "Her mother was due at my hospital. By then, my ground floor was a swimming pool."

You've never been so unpopular. You've never felt so awake.

Your phone buzzes. Sunny. "That was a dumb idea." You hold your breath. "Also the best one."

Ms. Okafor appears in the doorway with a mug of terrible coffee. She sets it in front of you. "No," she says.

"No what?"

"No. I'm not going easy on you anymore."

It's the first time you've seen her smile.

She takes the index card off the fridge and tucks it into your hand. "Keep it. The next one will need it more."

Then she says something that stays with you all week. "A good president isn't the one who's right. It's the one who asks who pays."


Day thirty. You walk out of the Oval Office in the same pajama pants you wore on day one. The general is waiting at the door. This time, you salute first. He grins, and salutes back a little slower than he should. The staff lines the hallway. Nobody claps. They just look you in the eye.

On the lawn, a drum rolls out. It's time to pick the next citizen. Ms. Okafor tears open an envelope and reads a name. Sunny Park, 17, Tulsa.

Your phone buzzes again. "Bet I last longer."

Would you take the job for 30 days, knowing that nobody would ever tell you no? Tell me in the comments. And would you have ordered that evacuation, with half the room telling you to wait?
