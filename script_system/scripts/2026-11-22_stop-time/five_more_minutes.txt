Your teacher's marker leaves her hand and stays in the air.

28 kids sit frozen mid-laugh. A paper plane hangs three feet above Nikhil's head. A sneeze is stuck halfway out of a girl in the back row, and it looks painful.

You walk down the aisle with a pack of star stickers. One on every forehead. 28 stars. Nikhil bet you $5 you couldn't prove it. You grab the paper plane, because you can, and carefully put it back.

At 12:04, the world starts again. 28 kids touch their foreheads at the same time. The marker clatters to the floor. "Who did this?" Everyone looks at you. You haven't moved from your seat.

Overnight, you got a new power. You can stop time. Everything freezes except you. It lasts as long as you want, and you hold it by staying awake.

Use it right, and you finish your homework before the bell. Use it more, and you learn an instrument in a weekend. Keep going, and you live a year while your friends live a month. And one afternoon, you'll have to hold the world still for longer than a human body can stay awake.

For today, you own 28 star stickers and one very suspicious teacher.

There are four rules, and you learn them fast. Everything stops but you. Whatever you touch wakes up for as long as you hold it, so you can open doors, eat a sandwich, write with a pen. People you touch wake up too, and they age along with you. The world restarts the second you fall asleep. And your body keeps going. One hour of stopped time is one hour of your life.

Physicists will tell you that if time really stopped, light would stop too, and you'd see nothing. Your power doesn't care.

Everyone says they need more time. You actually get it. You just pay for it with your own body.

You test it at lunch. The line has 40 people. You freeze everything, walk to the front, take a tray, and eat the whole thing sitting on the cafeteria roof with a view of 300 frozen students. It's the quietest lunch of your life. You start time again, and you're back in line, with pizza on your shirt.

Then it's homework. You stop time for one hour, finish three assignments, and read six library books on a beanbag. The librarian is frozen with a stamp in the air. You put a book on her desk. She never notices.

Your mom works nights as a nurse. She's the most tired person you know. For her birthday, you stop time for a whole day. You hang 400 balloons. You bake a cake that leans like a tower. You put a paper crown on the dog. Mom stands frozen in the doorway with her keys. At the end, you stand beside her, press start, and she walks into a party that wasn't there a second ago.

She cries. She says, "How long did that take you?" "A day." She laughs. She thinks you're joking.

Mom has a rule she repeats at dinner. "After 24 hours awake, you act like you've had a drink. I see it in the ER every night." You roll your eyes. You write it down anyway, somewhere in the back of your mind.

Every morning on the way to school, a construction foreman named Bruno hands you a paper cup of coffee over the fence. He's building a parking garage next door. Concrete floors, steel rods, a crane that never stops. "Don't tell your mother," he says. It's hot chocolate. You like him.

It stops being a game when you pick up the guitar.

You've wanted to play for years. You stop time for four months. That's a lot of practice. You sit on your bed with a metronome and nothing else in the world moving. Day one, your fingers bleed. Day four, they stop. Day nine, you finally hear a note you like. By day 120, a seven-minute piece comes out clean. Your body knows every day of it. The calluses are real.

You post a clip. The caption says, "Learned this in a weekend." It gets 14 million plays. A music conservatory sends an email. They want you at an audition. Tomorrow morning at nine is their only open slot.

Nikhil comes over that night. He sits on your floor, holding the strings you replaced. "You went from zero to concert in two days." "Yeah." "I've been practicing for three years." He doesn't say it angrily. He says it like he's checking if you can hear him.

Then your mom drags you to a doctor. Because you've grown an inch, your voice has dropped, and there's something strange on your chin. The doctor takes an X-ray of your hand. Doctors can tell your age from the growth plates in your bones. Teenagers have gaps there. Adults don't.

"You're 17?" "Yes." "Your hand says 19."

You've lived 14 extra months in a single year.

The bill shows up quietly.

First, the people. You're not the same age as your friends anymore. Nikhil still plays video games until 2 a.m. You've read forty books. You sit across from him and you don't know how to say it. You're bored of the things he loves. Mom counts your birthdays at dinner. "You're two years older than your birth certificate." She says it as a joke. She doesn't laugh.

Second, you. A normal teenager needs about 8 to 10 hours of sleep. You get three. Because "five more minutes" of stopped time becomes nine hours. You don't sleep at night anymore, because night is when the world is quietest. In the mirror, you see someone with gray under their eyes.

Third, the meaning. On your grandfather's 80th birthday, he tells a joke at the table, and his laugh fills the room. It's the best laugh you've ever heard. You stop time. You sit in the moment for three days. You walk around his open mouth. You study the crinkles around his eyes. You stay until your stomach hurts.

But a moment you stop is a moment you can only look at. His laugh doesn't continue. It never finishes. You press start, and it ends in a second. You had three days with it, and you didn't spend one of them with him.

The time you steal has nobody in it.

Eleven months later. It's the night before the audition. You've slept in real time for three weeks. You're practicing on a tailgate, in the parking lot behind school. Bruno waves from the garage. "Big day tomorrow?" "Nine sharp." "Don't be late." He gives you the hot chocolate.

At 3:40 p.m., you hear the sound.

Not a bang. A long, low groan, like a ship turning over. Then the floors of the garage fall. One after another, like pages. Dust rolls across the lot in a brown wave. Nine workers were inside.

You don't think. You stop time.

The dust hangs in the air like a gray curtain. A concrete slab the size of a bus is tilted a foot above Bruno's head. Beneath it, in a gap barely wider than a car door, he's frozen with his arm over his face.

You try to push the slab. It doesn't move. You try to lift it. It doesn't move. Frozen things stay frozen, unless you touch them. And if you touch the slab, it wakes up.

Rescuers say people in collapsed buildings survive in voids, in pockets of space between the layers. And before they lift anything, they build supports under it. Stacks of timber, crisscrossed like a game of Jenga. It's called cribbing. If you build it while time is frozen, the slab won't fall when it wakes up. It'll land on the wood.

But there are eleven slabs. And nine men under them. And hauling timber takes hours.

You count. About 30 hours of work. And the whole time, time must stay frozen. Which means you can't sleep.

After 17 hours awake, your brain works like it's had a couple of drinks. After 24, it's like being legally drunk. After a day and a half, many people start to see things that aren't there. A single microsleep, a blink of a few seconds you don't notice, will restart the world.

Everything you've stolen is due.

Mom's voice plays in your head. After a full day awake, you're not safe around anything sharp. You've already been up for 9 hours.

You could walk away. Go home, sleep, play the audition of your life tomorrow. Let the rescue crews do their best. Nobody would blame you for a thing.

Or you stay. And the audition, and the guitar, and the one thing you earned by yourself, are over.

You touch Bruno's arm.

He gasps. He drops to his knees. He looks around at a world made of silence. "What did you do?" "I stopped time." "You what?" "Bruno. I need you to tell me how to build cribbing."

He looks at the slab above his head. He looks at you. Then he nods, once. He's been doing this for 22 years.

It's the first time anyone has ever been in your time with you.

Hour one. You step under a hanging beam to reach a man. Bruno grabs your collar. "Never stand under anything you haven't propped." You nod. It's the first rule of the job, and you've broken it in five minutes.

You haul timber. Bruno shows you how to stack it. Four pieces, then four more, turned a quarter. Cribbing, he says, is the reason you can lift a slab without killing the person underneath. One by one, you wake the men and walk them out. Eight, seven, six. They stand in the parking lot in the frozen dust, quiet and confused, and one of them starts to pray. Then a big man named Marco wipes his face and picks up a plank. "Where do you want it?" One by one, the others do the same. Soon there are six pairs of hands. Every worker you wake gets older along with you, and not one of them asks to go home.

Hour 12. Your hands are bleeding again. Hour 17. You can't remember your phone number. Bruno slaps your cheeks. "Talk to me. Tell me about the guitar." You tell him. He tells you about his daughter. He tells you about the time he fell off a roof. He never stops talking.

Bruno hands you a cup of coffee, then a second. In 1964, a high school student named Randy Gardner stayed awake for 11 days as a science fair project. By the fourth day, he was seeing things that weren't there. "You're not him," Bruno says. "So drink up."

Hour 22. The floor tilts. You swear a man is standing beside you. He isn't.

Hour 26. You blink, and for one second, the world moves. A slab drops two inches. The timber groans. Everyone screams. You jerk awake and slam time still. The slab sits two inches lower, on a pile of wood, and doesn't fall.

"Don't you dare close your eyes," Bruno says. His voice is shaking.

Hour 31. The last man crawls out. You carry him through the dust. You put him on the grass beside the others.

Nine workers. Nobody missing.

You step away from the garage and close your eyes.

The world starts again. The slabs settle onto the wood like cats onto a lap. A siren hits the air. Paramedics run in from three directions, and not one of them understands why nine men are sitting calmly on the grass.

You sleep for 27 hours.

When you wake, the audition is long over. Mom is in the chair next to your bed. She's holding your hand. She doesn't say a word. She just keeps holding it.

That night, you carry your guitar to the parking lot. Bruno's crew is there with their families, folding chairs and paper cups. Nine workers. Wives. Kids. A dog. You sit on the tailgate and you play the seven-minute piece, in real time. Not one second of it is stopped. Nobody is frozen. Everyone is listening.

It's the first time you've played with people in the same minute as you.

Nikhil is in the back row. When you finish, he walks up and slaps a crumpled $5 bill on the tailgate. "I lost the bet. Worth it." You laugh until your eyes sting.

Grandpa is there too, in a folding chair with a blanket. Someone tells him what you did. He tells the same joke from his birthday. And this time, you listen to the whole laugh. Start to finish, at normal speed. It lasts four seconds. It's the best four seconds you've ever spent.

The conservatory gives you a second date, next month. You say you'll think about it. You don't need to.

A week later, an old man is waiting by the gate. He looks about 83. He wears a watch with no hands. "I stopped it once too," he says. "Forty-one years ago. I'm 83. Everyone I grew up with is 61." He smiles. "There are others. We meet on Thursdays."

He holds out a card. It says, five more minutes.

Would you want to stop time, knowing every minute you steal is a minute you live alone? Tell me in the comments. And would you have stayed awake for 31 hours to hold that building up?
